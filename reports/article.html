A Data-Backed Case Study

The Quiet Math of New York City's Restaurant Inspections

What 83,354 health inspections in 27,350 active New York City restaurants reveal about pests, plumbing, and what actually gets a kitchen shut down.

By Fahim Ahamed | LinkedIn | Portfolio
Data: NYC Department of Health published inspection records.

More than 85% of New York City's restaurant inspections end up with a grade A. More than half the restaurants needed a second try to get there.

Behind every A taped to a New York City storefront sits a public-health inspection report: a long checklist, a numerical score, and at the bottom, a single letter. City inspectors arrive at each kitchen unannounced, on a rolling cycle1, and tally violations against that checklist. The score is what counts. Higher number, worse kitchen1.

Most diners only see the card. The Department of Health publishes the data underneath on its open-data portal: every violation, every inspection, every closure. Roughly 83,354 inspections of 27,350 active restaurants sit in the file, every deli and wedding venue and late-night halal cart with a permit, each one a snapshot of a kitchen on a particular day.

DOHMH publishes inspections on a rolling roughly three-year window2. Months before 2022 are thinly populated; the program reached full monthly volume in mid-2022.

Look at all 83,354 inspections together and patterns show up that no single grade card reveals.

01Vermin is everywhere.

The most surprising number in the data shouldn't be: 33% of NYC inspections find evidence of mice, rats, roaches or flies. One in three. Borough to borough, the variation is mild: 35% in the Bronx, 30% on Staten Island. The borough numbers also reflect things the inspection data can't see: building age, sanitation infrastructure, and the rodent pressure a kitchen is operating against before its own practices enter the picture. At the neighbourhood level (NTAs with at least 200 inspections), the spread gets sharper: the worst sits at roughly 48% of inspections finding vermin, the cleanest at about 22%. The rate also has a calendar pulse: it peaks in November at 40% of inspections and bottoms out in April at about 27%.

If 1 in 3 inspections finds vermin, vermin can't be what closes a restaurant. Most of those 83,354 visits ended with the kitchen still open. The question is what does.

02The closure cliff.

NYC inspectors close a restaurant on the spot in only 1.74% of inspections. That headline rate hides a sharper truth: when a few specific violations appear, the closure probability doesn't creep up. It jumps.

Even the live-roach and live-rat codes only push closure risk to three to five times the baseline. They're bad, but they aren't, by themselves, what closes kitchens.

Inspections containing 04F (food area contaminated by sewage) end in a closure 34% of the time, nineteen times the baseline rate. 05A (inadequate sewage disposal) and 05E (missing or improper toilet facilities) are nearly as severe. These are the violations that take a kitchen from "could open tomorrow" to "is closing today."

It's the plumbing. A live rat sighting raises your chance of being closed by about 5×. A sewage problem raises it 14 to 20×. Everyone worries about rats. The data says worry about the pipes.

These inspections are rarely single-issue events. Only 596 inspections contained one of the three top sewage codes in the window, but those had a median score of 52 (versus 13 for everyone else) and 6 violations apiece (versus three). The plumbing failure is the marker, not the sole cause. 100% also flagged at least one critical food-safety violation.

A handful of recognizable names appear in the data at the catastrophic end of the spectrum. A Le Pain Quotidien location in Manhattan posted a score of 168 in July 2023 with a sewage citation. A Manhattan % Arabica hit 163 in March 2026 under the same code. A Brooklyn restaurant recorded a catastrophic 200 in April 2026, with eighteen violations, eleven of them critical, and sewage among them. Each of these kitchens was closed that day. The Le Pain inspection is from mid-2023; the others are from 2026. These are individual snapshots from across the three-year window, not a current scandal.

The sewage-cliff inspections aren't concentrated in one part of the city. They're scattered across all five boroughs. 03The 13-point ceiling.

Inspection scores pile up at exactly 12 and 13, the two highest scores that still earn an A. Score 12 is the single most common score in the data (9,858 inspections); score 13 is second (8,476). Together they account for 18,334 inspections.

And then, at score 14, the count collapses. Only 433 inspections land there, one point above the A/B grade boundary. The combined pile-up at 12 and 13 is roughly 42× the count at 14, despite the bins being one point apart.

This is not random. The grade boundary lives exactly between 13 and 14. Every inspection scoring 0–13 received an A; every one scoring 14–27 received a B. With rare exceptions, no boundary crossings either way. A one-point swing on the worksheet is the difference between a green A taped to the window and a yellow B that stays up until re-inspection.

Re-inspections, whose purpose is to recover the A, show the same discontinuity. A re-inspection landing at 13 is roughly 20× as common as one landing at 14. The data can't say whether this reflects restaurants cleaning up just enough or inspectors rounding marginal cases to the safe side of the line. Most likely both. Either way, the A is a target, not a description.

04Most kitchens bounce back.

Most NYC kitchen closures are over in a week. After a sewage-cliff closure, the median wait until the next inspection is just 6 days, versus about 70 days for a routine re-inspection, and 69% of those follow-ups end with DOHMH formally re-opening the restaurant. The system is built to give kitchens a fast second chance, and most take it.

And it isn't just sewage closures. The transformation across the board is dramatic. Of the 4,376 restaurants that failed an initial cycle inspection in the C zone (score 28 or above), the median score on the re-inspection drops 22 points in about two months, from 37 down to 13, the highest score that still earns an A. More than half (58%) recover to an A on the next visit.

A small minority defy the pattern. 8.0% of these restaurants got worse on the next inspection. Another 18% of sewage-cliff closures were re-closed at the very next visit. The recovery system works for most, but it leaks: some kitchens stay in the failure cycle.

05NYC's food map is sliced into pockets.

NYC has restaurants in 194 distinct Neighborhood Tabulation Areas, the city's official mid-sized geography between ZIP code and borough. Pizza lives in 186 of them. Its top five neighbourhoods together account for only 12% of all NYC pizza restaurants. Pizza is, statistically, everywhere.

Other cuisines aren't. The top 5 neighbourhoods for Bangladeshi restaurants hold 57% of them; Elmhurst alone holds 18%. Over a third of NYC's Bangladeshi restaurants sit in just two adjacent Queens neighbourhoods.

The five coloured cuisines on the map all sit toward the top of the concentration ranking. Korean is the standout multi-anchor cuisine, split almost evenly between Flushing's Korean community (about 94 restaurants) and Midtown Manhattan's commercial K-town (about 93). Bangladeshi, Jewish/Kosher, and Eastern European each track one of NYC's best-known immigrant neighbourhoods. French clusters in Manhattan's historically high-income districts. And Pizza, the grey layer, is the counter-example: a cuisine so universal that its city-wide footprint is the city itself.

Two more extremes sit in the underlying numbers, beyond what the map alone shows. Midtown serves food from 65 distinct cuisines, basically every kind of food NYC offers packed into one neighbourhood. Tribeca, much smaller, manages 61. At the other end, some neighbourhoods are nearly mono-cuisine: 60% of East Flatbush's restaurants are Caribbean, 53% of downtown Flushing's are Chinese, 51% of Midwood's are Jewish/Kosher. NYC's food map is fractal: radically diverse in some places, radically concentrated in others.

One last fact, about how universal pizza is (and Chinese). The data show pizza in 186 NYC neighbourhoods, and Chinese food in exactly the same 186. By raw count Chinese is actually the bigger of the two: 2,276 restaurants to pizza's 1,597. The neighbourhoods without either are mostly park, zoo, and stadium areas (Central Park, the Bronx Zoo, Flushing Meadows / Citi Field, the Dyker Beach Golf Course), plus a handful of small residential pockets the data simply doesn't capture. The only NYC neighbourhoods without pizza or Chinese are mostly the ones nobody actually lives in.

06The summer effect.

Heat changes what inspectors find. The cold-food code (02G, "cold food held above 41°F") appears in 27% of summer inspections, up from 19% in winter, a 1.42× lift consistent with walk-in coolers struggling against warmer ambient temperatures.

The opposite pattern shows up on the hot-food side. The 02B code ("hot food held below 140°F") actually drops in summer, from 20% of winter inspections to 17% in summer. Same physics, opposite sign: warm kitchens help hot food stay hot.

On-the-spot closures track the season too: 2.02% of summer inspections end in a closure, against 1.59% in winter, a 1.27× jump. August is the riskiest month for a NYC kitchen (2.69%); May the safest (1.36%). The city itself slows down: roughly 17% fewer inspections per summer month than per winter one. But the ones that happen find more, on every dimension summer plausibly affects. Same kitchen, different month, different odds.

07What an ‘A’ really means.

The grade card suggests a simple sort: clean restaurants get an A, dirty ones don't. The data underneath suggests something else.

The A on the door is a snapshot of the most recent visit, not a long-term record. Only 993 NYC restaurants (about 4%) have a perfectly clean three-year history. The remaining 96% have been cited for something serious at least once, then cleaned up enough to keep or earn back the grade.

Put the numbers side by side. 87% of graded inspections end in an A. But 57% of restaurants needed a re-inspection to get there. And scores pile up 42× more at 12–13 than at 14, right at the grade boundary. The A is less a description of kitchen quality than an outcome the system is structured to produce.

The chance of a second visit varies sharply by what kind of restaurant you run. Bagels/Pretzels restaurants are re-inspected 72% of the time after a cycle inspection; New American only 42%.

The pattern at the ends of this chart isn't about cuisine. It tracks two structural factors the data can't fully separate: kitchen complexity (a counter pulling pre-made ingredients has fewer ways to fail than a multi-station prep kitchen) and geography (cuisines that concentrate in higher-income parts of the city absorb fewer citations). The top of the chart skews independent, full-prep, and outer-borough; the bottom skews counter-service, limited-menu, often chain-operated.

What the A tells you: this kitchen has been inspected recently, has cleaned up whatever was off, and is not currently in active enforcement. That's not nothing. But it is a much smaller claim than the card makes it look.

The grade card and the data behind it.

A few patterns repeat across the data, and they fit together into one picture.

Vermin is everywhere, and it is not what gets a kitchen shut down. When kitchens do close, plumbing is usually the trigger, and by then the rest of the kitchen has usually fallen apart too. And the borough-level numbers read in headlines smooth over differences between neighbours several times larger than the differences between boroughs.

These patterns are less about which NYC restaurants are clean and more about how the grading system, the inspection cycle, and the kitchens being graded interact to produce the letters on the windows.

Key takeaways

  1. Scores cluster right under the A/B line. Scores 12 and 13 are the two most common in the data, the highest still earning an A; a score of 14 is roughly 20× rarer than 13, one point lower.
  2. Plumbing closes kitchens. Pests almost never do. A live-rat citation raises closure risk about 5×; a sewage code raises it 14 to 20×.
  3. Most failed kitchens recover. After a C-zone failure, the median score drops 22 points in about two months; 58% are back to an A on the very next visit.
  4. NYC's food map is sliced into pockets. The top 5 neighbourhoods for some cuisines hold over 57% of all of that cuisine's restaurants in the city. Universal cuisines like Pizza spread across 186 of the city's 194 neighbourhoods, so does Chinese.

What this data cannot tell us

DOHMH publishes a rolling roughly three-year window of inspection records2. Older inspections drop off the back end. The data's earliest published year (2007) is a sliver: fewer than 10 inspections per year through 2014, and under 300 per year through 2021. Everything in this article uses inspections from mid-2022 onward, when monthly volume reached its current level.

The city also publishes records only for restaurants that are still in active status2. Two different things in this article are called "closure," and they aren't the same:

  • The on-the-spot closures discussed in sections 2 and 4 are a regulatory action DOHMH takes during an inspection. They are temporary. Section 4 shows that most of these restaurants are re-opened within days. The kitchens involved are still in the active file.
  • Permanent business closures, where a restaurant loses its permit or goes out of business and is removed from the active roll, are not in this file at all. Whatever their final inspection looked like, the record leaves with them.

The closure rates in this article therefore describe what happens to restaurants that are still operating today. They cannot describe what happens to the ones that didn't survive long enough to stay on the active roll.
