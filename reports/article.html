A Data Case Study

The Quiet Math of New York's Restaurant Inspections

What 83,354 health inspections in 27,350 active New York City restaurants reveal about pests, plumbing, and what actually gets a kitchen shut down.

By Fahim Ahamed · LinkedIn · Portfolio
Data: NYC Department of Health published inspection records.

New York City has more restaurants than any other American city. Roughly 27,350 active food establishments at last count, spread across five boroughs and an even longer list of cuisines. Behind that visible city is an invisible one: a small army of public-health inspectors who arrive at each kitchen, unannounced, on a rolling cycle. They tally violations against a long checklist, add up a score, and assign the letter grade that ends up taped to the front window. Higher score, worse kitchen. Anything from 0 to 13 earns an A; 14 to 27 a B; 28 and above a C.

Most diners only ever see the grade card. What they don't see is the underlying data: every violation, every inspection, every closure. The city quietly publishes all of it on its open-data portal. That is a generous slice of the city's food life. Every deli, every wedding venue, every late-night Halal cart that has a permit, photographed in a different moment of order or disorder by someone with a clipboard.

How much data, and from when? DOHMH publishes inspections on a rolling roughly three-year window. Records older than that drop off the back end as new ones come in. In practice, the months before 2022 are very thinly populated. The program's published output only reached full monthly volume through the back half of that year.

Looking at all of those visits at once produces a portrait that no single grade card can. The picture is, by turns, reassuring and alarming. Most inspections turn out roughly the same. Most kitchens recover from a bad day. A handful of very specific failures, however, end the inspection on the spot.

01Vermin is everywhere.

The single most surprising number from the data is the one that shouldn't be. 33% of New York inspections find evidence of mice, rats, roaches or flies. One in three. Borough to borough, the variation is mild: 35% in the Bronx, 30% on Staten Island. Neighborhood to neighborhood, the spread is sharper. The worst residential or commercial slice of the city sees a pest violation in roughly 48% of inspections, the cleanest in about 22%.

That rate is high enough to mean roughly nothing on its own. If 1 in 3 inspections finds vermin, then vermin can't be what closes a restaurant. Most of those 83,354 visits ended with the kitchen still open. The question is what does.

02The closure cliff.

Most violations don't shut you down. A handful do. New York City inspectors close a restaurant on the spot in only 1.74% of inspections (fewer than two in every hundred). That headline rate hides a sharper truth. When a few specific violations appear, the closure probability doesn't just creep up. It jumps.

The pattern is striking. Vermin codes, even the live-roach code and the live-rat code, push closure risk up by roughly three to five times the baseline. They are bad. They are not, by themselves, the reason kitchens get shut down. The codes that do shut kitchens down are about plumbing.

Inspections containing 04F (food area contaminated by sewage) end in a closure 34% of the time, nineteen times the baseline rate. 05A (inadequate sewage disposal) and 05E (missing or improper toilet facilities) are nearly as severe. These are the violations that take a kitchen from "could open tomorrow" to "is closing today."

It's the plumbing. A live rat sighting raises your chance of being closed by about 5×. A sewage problem raises it by 15 to 19×. Vermin makes the news; sewage takes the keys.

The deeper read is that these inspections are rarely single-issue events. Only 596 inspections contained one of the three top sewage codes over the three-year window, but those inspections had a median score of 52 (versus 13 for everyone else) and a median of 6 violations apiece (versus three). The plumbing failure is the marker, not the sole cause. When a kitchen reaches that point, a lot has already gone wrong. 100% of those inspections also flagged at least one critical food-safety violation.

A handful of recognizable names appear in the data at the catastrophic end of the spectrum. A Le Pain Quotidien location in Manhattan posted a score of 168 in July 2023 with a sewage citation. A Manhattan % Arabica hit 163 in March 2026 under the same code. A small Brooklyn restaurant called Jay & Son Latin Flavor recorded a score of 200 this past spring, with eighteen violations, eleven of them critical, and sewage among them. Each of these kitchens was closed that day.

The sewage-cliff inspections aren't concentrated in one part of the city. They're scattered across all five boroughs, in every neighborhood the published data covers. 03The 13-point ceiling.

Zoom in on the lower end of the distribution and something strange falls out. Inspection scores pile up at exactly 12 and 13, the two highest scores that still earn an A. Score 12 alone is the single most common score in the entire dataset, with 9,858 inspections. Score 13 is the second most common, with 8,476. Together those two integers account for 18,334 inspections.

And then, at score 14, the count collapses. Only 433 inspections land there, one point above the A/B grade boundary. The combined pile-up at 12 and 13 is roughly 42× the count at 14, despite the bins being one point apart.

This is not random. The grade-card boundary lives exactly between 13 and 14. In the published data, every single inspection scoring 0 through 13 received an A. Every single inspection scoring 14 through 27 received a B. A one-point swing on the inspector's worksheet is the difference between a green A taped to the front window and a yellow B that has to stay up until you re-inspect.

Re-inspections, whose entire purpose is to recover the A, show the same discontinuity. A re-inspection landing at 13 is roughly 20× as common as one landing at 14. The data does not say whether the pile-up reflects restaurants cleaning up just enough or inspectors rounding marginal cases down to the safe side of the line. Most likely it reflects both. Either way, the A is a target, not a description.

04What happens next.

Closures look severe but they're not, in most cases, permanent. The city moves fast. After a sewage-cliff closure, the median wait until the next inspection is just 6 days, compared with about 70 days for a routine cycle re-inspection. Roughly 69% of those follow-ups end with DOHMH formally re-opening the restaurant. The kitchen, usually, gets cleaned up.

A broader version of the same story holds for any restaurant that fails its initial cycle inspection in the C zone (score 28 or above): 4,376 such failures in the window. Median score on the re-inspection drops by 22 points, from 37 all the way down to 13. More than half (58%) recover all the way to an A on the very next visit.

A small minority defy the pattern. 8.0% of these restaurants actually got worse on the next inspection. Another 18% of sewage-cliff closures were re-closed at the very next visit. The recovery system is real, but it leaks at the bottom of the distribution. The same kitchens keep failing.

05The cuisine spectrum.

Not every cuisine fares the same. Indian restaurants are closed during inspection in 5.0% of visits; French restaurants in just 0.49%. That's a roughly 10× gap from one end of the menu to the other.

Read this chart carefully. The cuisines at the top (Indian, Middle Eastern, Caribbean, Chinese) are heavily concentrated outside Manhattan. The cuisines at the bottom (French, Italian, Irish, Hamburgers) are disproportionately Manhattan establishments. We can't tell, from this data alone, whether what we're seeing is a property of the cuisines themselves or a property of the neighborhoods they live in. Most likely it's some of both. The confound is the finding.

Where cuisines clearly do differ is in how they fail. Each cuisine has a signature pattern of citations: codes that show up more (or less) often than the city-wide baseline. A few examples: Chinese kitchens are over-represented on the rats and mice codes. Coffee shops over-cite for missing pest-management contracts. 06The smaller-than-borough story.

The familiar five-borough frame turns out to hide most of the interesting variation. Sort New York's 51 City Council districts by their closure rate and the spread is dramatic: 3.55% at the top, 0.45% at the bottom. That is a 7.8× gap. The familiar borough comparison covers about a 2× range; sub-borough geography covers nearly four times that.

"Brooklyn" and "Manhattan" are large coalitions. Inside them are districts that look almost nothing alike. A quiet reminder that when we talk about the food landscape of a city, we are nearly always talking about the food landscape of a few square blocks.

What this data cannot tell us

Two cautions matter for anyone who reads further into these numbers. The first is that DOHMH publishes a rolling roughly three-year window of inspection records. Earlier inspections drop off the back end; the dataset's headline 2007 minimum date is a decorative sliver. Everything in this article covers calendar 2022 onward, the period in which the city's inspection program was running at full monthly volume.

The second caution is more consequential. The city only publishes records for restaurants that are still in active status. Establishments that permanently closed (including any that closed after enforcement and never reopened) drop out of the file entirely. The city's closure rate, then, undercounts the most consequential closure of all. The numbers in this article describe what happens to surviving restaurants. They do not describe what happens to the ones that don't come back.

Source

NYC OpenData · DOHMH Restaurant Inspection Results (dataset id 43nn-pn8j), snapshot of 2026-05-30.

Method

Raw violation-grain CSV → one row per inspection in src/build_inspections.py. All findings and charts built by src/build_article.py. Restricted to inspection year ≥ 2022 and the five real boroughs. The dataset also contains a small amount of 2007–2021 residue (≤300 rows per year), which has been excluded. Charts powered by Plotly.js.

Caveats

"Active restaurants" means any CAMIS that appears in the published file; permanently closed restaurants are not represented. The cuisine analysis is not adjusted for the cuisine/geography confound described in §5. Re-inspection pairs are bounded to ≤180 days so the next cycle's initial inspection is not counted as the previous one's re-inspection.
